Problem Set 1 Solutions

1. Counting sort

The output contains exactly the input elements. For each value $x$, the counter $C[x]$ starts at zero and is incremented once for every occurrence of $x$ in the input. Thus $C[x]$ counts exactly how many times $x$ appears. Since the algorithm outputs $x$ exactly $C[x]$ times, every value has the same number of occurrences in the output as in the input.

The output is sorted. The algorithm visits $0,1,\ldots,n-1$ in increasing order and outputs all copies of each value before moving to the next. Consequently, the output is in nondecreasing order.

The running time is $O(n)$. All input values, array indices, and counts are between $0$ and $n$, so each fits in one machine word. In the word-RAM model, reading or writing an array entry, adding one to a word, and comparing two words each take constant time. Initializing a counter requires one write. Updating $C[a_i]$ requires reading $a_i$, reading $C[a_i]$, adding one, and writing the result back: a constant number of constant-time operations. Outputting a value requires one word write. The increments and comparisons used to control the loops also take constant time.

Initializing the $n$ counters takes $O(n)$ time. Counting the input also takes $O(n)$ time, since each of the $n$ elements requires one counter update. Finally, the output phase visits $n$ counters and writes

$$ \sum_{x=0}^{n-1} C[x] = n $$

elements in total. Since visiting a counter and writing an output element each take constant time, this phase takes $O(n+n)=O(n)$ time. Adding the times for the three phases gives $O(n)$ total time.

2. Harmonic series

Upper bound. Replacing each denominator by the largest power of two not exceeding it can only increase the sum. Thus

$$ \begin{aligned} H_n &= 1+\frac12+\frac13+\cdots+\frac{1}{n} \\ &\le 1+ \underbrace{\frac12+\frac12}_{1}+ \underbrace{\frac14+\frac14+\frac14+\frac14}_{1} +\cdots, \end{aligned} $$

where the right-hand side is stopped after $n$ terms. Each complete block has $2^k$ terms equal to $1/2^k$, so it sums to $1$. The last block may be incomplete, in which case it contributes at most $1$. There are $1+\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$ blocks, hence

$$ H_n\le 1+\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor\le 2\log_2 n \qquad (n\ge 2). $$

Therefore $H_n=O(\log_2 n)$.

Lower bound. Replacing each denominator by the smallest power of two at least as large as it can only decrease the sum. Thus

$$ \begin{aligned} H_n &= 1+\frac12+\frac13+\cdots+\frac{1}{n} \\ &\ge 1+ \underbrace{\frac12}_{1/2}+ \underbrace{\frac14+\frac14}_{1/2}+ \underbrace{\frac18+\frac18+\frac18+\frac18}_{1/2} +\cdots, \end{aligned} $$

again stopping after $n$ terms. Each complete block after the initial $1$ sums to $1/2$. These blocks end at indices $2,4,8,\ldots$, so there are $\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$ complete blocks. Discarding any final incomplete block gives

$$ H_n\ge 1+\frac12\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor \ge 1+\frac12(\log_2 n-1) \ge \frac12\log_2 n. $$

Therefore $H_n=\Omega(\log_2 n)$. Together with the upper bound, this proves $H_n=\Theta(\log_2 n)$.

4. Asymptotic behavior of functions

(a) The ordering is

$$ (f_1,f_5,f_2,f_3,f_4). $$

Since $\log_2(n^{2019})=2019\log_2 n$, we have $f_2(n)=2019n^2\log_2 n$. The ratios of consecutive functions satisfy

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{f_1(n)}{f_5(n)} &= \frac{(\log_2 n)^{2018}}{n}\longrightarrow 0, \\ \frac{f_5(n)}{f_2(n)} &= \frac{1}{2019n}\longrightarrow 0, \\ \frac{f_2(n)}{f_3(n)} &= \frac{2019\log_2 n}{n}\longrightarrow 0, \\ \frac{f_3(n)}{f_4(n)} &= \frac{n^3}{(2.019)^n}\longrightarrow 0. \end{aligned} $$

Here we use that any fixed power of $\log_2 n$ grows more slowly than $n$, and any fixed power of $n$ grows more slowly than $b^n$ for a constant $b>1$. Each ratio tends to zero, so no two functions have the same $\Theta$ growth rate.

(b) The ordering is

$$ \bigl(\{f_2,f_5\},f_3,f_1,f_4\bigr). $$

First,

$$ \binom{n}{3}=\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{6}=\Theta(n^3), $$

so $f_2$ and $f_5$ belong in the same group.

Stirling’s formula states that $n!=\Theta\bigl(\sqrt{n}(n/e)^n\bigr)$. Applying it to $n!$ and $(n/2)!$, for even $n$ we obtain

$$ \binom{n}{n/2} =\frac{n!}{((n/2)!)^2} =\Theta\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}(n/e)^n}{(n/2)(n/(2e))^n}\right) =\Theta\left(\frac{2^n}{\sqrt{n}}\right). $$

This grows faster than $n^3$, since $n^{7/2}/2^n\to 0$, and more slowly than $2^n$, since $1/\sqrt{n}\to 0$. Finally, Stirling’s formula also gives

$$ \frac{2^n}{n!} =\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{2e}{n}\right)^n\right) \longrightarrow 0, $$

so $n!$ grows faster than $2^n$.

5. Dynamic-array append

If there is space left, an append takes $O(1)$ time. If the array is full with $k$ elements, we allocate and initialize $2k$ slots, copy the $k$ elements, and insert the new one. By the assumptions, this takes $O(k)$ time.

Let $c\ge 1$ be the initial capacity, and assume $c=O(1)$. Successive doublings cost $O(c),O(2c),O(4c),\ldots$. The $m$ appends require at most $L=\lceil\log_2 m\rceil$ doublings, since $c2^L\ge m$.

Checking for space and writing the new elements costs $O(m)$ in total. Adding the initial allocation and the doubling costs gives

$$ \begin{aligned} T(m) &= O\left(m+c+c\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}2^i\right) \\ &= O(m+c2^L) \\ &= O(m), \end{aligned} $$

Here the geometric sum is $2^L-1$ (zero when $L=0$), and $2^L<2m$. Since $c=O(1)$, the total time is $O(m)$, giving $O(1)$ amortized time per append.

6. Merge sort

These proofs adapt Emory University’s Merge Sort Analysis, Proofs #2 and #3.

Proof 1: repeated substitution.

Let $T(n)$ be the maximum running time over nonempty inputs with at most $n$ elements. First suppose $n$ is a power of two. Each recursive half then has at most $n/2$ elements. Since splitting and merging take linear time, we can choose a constant $C>0$ such that

$$ T(1)\le C, \qquad T(n)\le 2T(n/2)+Cn \quad (n\ge 2). $$

For $n\ge 4$, the first two substitutions give

$$ \begin{aligned} T(n) &\le 2T(n/2)+Cn \\ &\le 2\bigl(2T(n/4)+C(n/2)\bigr)+Cn \\ &= 4T(n/4)+2Cn. \end{aligned} $$

Each substitution doubles the number of subproblems and adds another $Cn$ to the bound. After $j$ substitutions, where $0\le j\le\log_2 n$,

$$ T(n)\le 2^jT(n/2^j)+jCn. $$

Setting $j=\log_2 n$ leaves subproblems of size $1$, so

$$ T(n)\le nT(1)+Cn\log_2 n \le Cn(\log_2 n+1) =O(n\log_2 n). $$

For an arbitrary input size $n$, let $N=2^{\lceil\log_2 n\rceil}$, so $n\le N<2n$. Since $T(n)$ includes all inputs of size at most $n$, we have $T(n)\le T(N)$. The bound just proved gives $T(n)=O(N\log_2 N)=O(n\log_2 n)$.

Proof 2: induction.

Let $T(n)$ be the worst-case running time on an array of $n$ elements. Merge sort recursively sorts two halves and then merges them. By the assumptions, there is a constant $C>0$ such that $T(1)\le C$ and, for $n\ge 2$,

$$ T(n)\le T(\lfloor n/2\rfloor)+T(\lceil n/2\rceil)+Cn. $$

The $Cn$ term accounts for splitting and merging. We prove by strong induction on $n\ge 1$ that

$$ T(n)\le Cn\bigl(\lceil\log_2 n\rceil+1\bigr). $$

Base case. For $n=1$, the proposed bound is $C$, so it follows from $T(1)\le C$.

Inductive step. Let $n\ge 2$ and assume the bound holds for every smaller positive input size. Write $a=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ and $b=\lceil n/2\rceil$. Both $a$ and $b$ are less than $n$, and $a+b=n$.

Since $n\le 2^{\lceil\log_2 n\rceil}$, each half has at most $2^{\lceil\log_2 n\rceil-1}$ elements. Thus $\lceil\log_2 a\rceil\le\lceil\log_2 n\rceil-1$ and $\lceil\log_2 b\rceil\le\lceil\log_2 n\rceil-1$. The induction hypothesis therefore gives $T(a)\le Ca\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$ and $T(b)\le Cb\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$. Substituting these bounds into the recurrence,

$$ \begin{aligned} T(n) &\le T(a)+T(b)+Cn \\ &\le Ca\lceil\log_2 n\rceil+Cb\lceil\log_2 n\rceil+Cn \\ &= Cn\bigl(\lceil\log_2 n\rceil+1\bigr). \end{aligned} $$

This completes the induction. For $n\ge 2$, we have $\lceil\log_2 n\rceil+1\le\log_2 n+2\le 3\log_2 n$. Hence $T(n)=O(n\log_2 n)$.
